A recent study by the *Universitat de València* (UV) has highlighted **health risks** associated with a species of mackerel commonly regarded as healthy: the **Atlantic chub mackerel** (*Scomber colias*), which is often confused on the market with Atlantic mackerel.

**What has been detected**

The scientists analysed 250 specimens of **Atlantic chub mackerel** (125 from the Atlantic and 125 from the Mediterranean), bought from different supermarkets in and around the city of Valencia. They found ***Anisakis*** type I larvae —the main causative agent of **anisakiasis**— and other parasites (*Contracaecum*, *Hysterothylacium* and the acanthocephalan *Rhadinorhynchus pristis*) which, although they are not usually dangerous to health, spoil the appearance and texture of the fish.

**Differences between species and origin of the fish**

**Two species of mackerel** are sold in Spain: the Atlantic chub mackerel (*Scomber colias*) and the Atlantic mackerel (*Scomber scombrus*). Despite their biological differences, these species are often confused by both consumers and sellers.

The study points out that the presence of **Anisakis** varies depending on the geographical origin of the fish: the prevalence of *Anisakis* type I was higher in specimens from the Atlantic Ocean than in those from the Mediterranean.

The researchers therefore draw attention to the importance of **clearly distinguishing** the species of mackerel being bought and its origin, preferably by checking the label on the packaging or at the counter.

**Risks and health recommendations**

**Anisakiasis** is a disease caused by eating fish infected with **Anisakis** larvae, particularly if it is eaten raw or is not properly cooked/frozen.

To reduce the risk, the researchers point to the preventive measures of the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA): cooking fish to a minimum internal temperature of **60 °C for at least 1 minute**, or freezing it at **–20 °C for at least 24 hours** (preferably 3 days) or at **–35 °C for at least 15 hours**, if it is to be eaten raw or semi-raw.

**Conclusion**

Although mackerel —including the chub mackerel variety— is valued for its nutritional benefits, eating it without precautions can involve a risk of parasitisation. According to the authors, Atlantic mackerel is even more heavily parasitised than chub mackerel, and the risk is lower if the fish comes from the Mediterranean; they therefore advise taking preventive measures: it is **essential to know the species**, its **origin** and to apply **food safety measures** correctly.
