A recent study by the *Universitat de València* (UV) has highlighted **health risks** associated with a species of mackerel commonly regarded as healthy: the **Atlantic chub mackerel** (*Scomber colias*), which is often confused on the market with Atlantic mackerel.

**What has been detected**

The scientists analysed 250 specimens of **Atlantic chub mackerel**, bought at random from different Spanish supermarkets. The results were worrying: around **31%** of the fish examined contained live parasite larvae, including members of the genus ***Anisakis*** —the main causative agent of **anisakiasis**. Other types of parasites were also found which, although they pose no direct risk to health, can compromise the quality, texture and flavour of the fish.

**Differences between species and origin of the fish**

**Two species of mackerel** are sold in Spain: the Atlantic chub mackerel (*Scomber colias*) and the Atlantic mackerel (*Scomber scombrus*). Despite their biological differences, these species are often confused by both consumers and sellers.

The study points out that the prevalence of **Anisakis** larvae and other parasites can vary depending on the geographical origin of the fish: specimens caught in the Atlantic Ocean showed a higher level of parasitisation than those caught in the Mediterranean.

The researchers therefore draw attention to the importance of **clearly distinguishing** the species of mackerel being bought and its origin, preferably by checking the label on the packaging or at the counter.

**Risks and health recommendations**

**Anisakiasis** is a disease caused by eating fish infected with **Anisakis** larvae, particularly if it is eaten raw or is not properly cooked/frozen.

To reduce the risk, experts recommend applying the preventive measures laid down in European legislation: cooking fish to a minimum internal temperature of **60 °C for at least 1 minute**, or freezing it at **–20 °C for at least 24 hours** (preferably 3 days) or at **–35 °C for at least 15 hours**, if it is to be eaten raw or semi-raw.

**Conclusion**

Although mackerel —including the chub mackerel variety— is valued for its nutritional benefits, eating it without precautions can involve a risk of parasitisation. This study warns that, in the current context, it is not enough to assume that all oily fish is safe: it is **essential to know the species**, its **origin** and to apply **food safety measures** correctly.
