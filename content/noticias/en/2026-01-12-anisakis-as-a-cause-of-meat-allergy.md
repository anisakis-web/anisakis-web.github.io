A recent study by Spanish researchers has provided new clues as to how the parasite *Anisakis* —known for causing anisakiosis after eating raw or undercooked fish— may also **act as a trigger of food allergies in humans**.

Although we traditionally associate anisakis with digestive illnesses following ingestion, this parasite appears to play another, lesser-known role: **it can trigger immune responses in predisposed people**, even after indirect exposure.

**What happens in the body?**

The parasite produces complex proteins (glycoproteins) containing a component called **α-Gal**, which the human immune system recognises as foreign. When these proteins come into contact with the body, they can stimulate the production of specific IgE antibodies. This reaction is similar to the one that occurs in some food allergies and can give rise to what is known as **α-Gal syndrome (AGS)**, a form of food allergy.

This means that a sensitised person could not only **experience symptoms when eating infected fish**, but in rare cases symptoms could also appear after eating other foods containing this epitope, or after repeated exposure to similar proteins from other sources.

**Allergic manifestations: from mild to severe**

When the immune system overreacts to these proteins, various symptoms may appear, varying in intensity:

- **Mild to moderate reactions:** such as urticaria, itchy skin or swelling (angioedema).
- **Gastrointestinal symptoms:** abdominal pain, diarrhoea, nausea or vomiting.
- **Severe reactions:** in some cases, **anaphylaxis** may develop, a severe allergic response that can affect the cardiovascular and respiratory systems and be life-threatening if not treated immediately.

The severity of the reaction usually depends on the amount of α-Gal-containing proteins to which the individual was exposed and on their level of sensitisation.

**What does this research tell us?**

This study is one of the first to **demonstrate the presence of these allergenic proteins in *Anisakis*** and how **they can activate specific immune responses in people with allergies**. Its findings open up new avenues for better understanding how parasites can influence allergic diseases beyond classic infections.
